A HUSKYDADDY NOVEL STUDY

Coming of Age and Moral Responsibility

When does understanding create a duty to act?

Essential question

When does understanding create a duty to act?

Topic → argument

Coming of age is a topic about development; moral responsibility concerns obligations toward others. A theme statement should connect development to a change in judgement, feeling, or action. Turning Twelve is a public milestone, but the novel asks whether receiving an adult role is the same as learning to evaluate what adults do.

Evidence note: Novel events below are paraphrased. Chapter numbers locate the relevant events; use page numbers from your own edition in formal MLA writing. Interpretations are arguments to test, not verified statements of authorial intention.

Two defensible arguments

  • Moral maturity involves testing inherited expectations against consequences, even when doing so threatens a person’s belonging.
  • Responsibility can require either leaving a harmful system or staying to help change it; the ethical value lies in the purpose and consequences of the choice.

Development across the novel

Jonas moves from trusting adult decisions to evaluating their effects. Pain and emotional experience make familiar behaviour look different. The Giver and Jonas eventually choose different roles in a shared attempt at change.

Institutional adulthood precedes independent judgement · Chapters 1–8

The Ceremony of Twelve organizes a recognized transition into work. Jonas initially worries about how well the Elders will choose, not whether he should judge their system. Exposition establishes maturity as a role conferred by authority.

His later development complicates that model because new experience makes approved conduct questionable. Direct praise at selection cannot substitute for the process of learning how to use intelligence and courage when a trusted rule produces an unacceptable consequence.

Feeling changes what ordinary behaviour means · Chapters 15–17

The battlefield memory gives Jonas knowledge that makes the war game painful. This contrast shows why moral development cannot be measured only by learning definitions. An apparently harmless activity becomes difficult because he can imagine real suffering.

Yet the disagreement with Asher reveals another limitation: concern does not automatically become communication. Limited viewpoint helps readers understand Jonas without requiring the conclusion that everyone outside his experience is intentionally cruel. Growth creates responsibilities for explaining as well as objecting.

A mentor can learn a new obligation · Chapters 18–20

The Giver’s hesitation over Rosemary’s history is understandable in light of loss, but his relationship with Jonas helps move him toward a plan. His decision to remain and guide others suggests development even in an older, knowledgeable person. Character contrast makes different actions serve a shared ethical purpose.

Leaving is not automatically courageous, and staying is not automatically obedience. Readers must examine what need each person addresses and what burden each accepts.

Acting before perfect readiness · Chapters 21–22

Jonas abandons the prepared schedule when Gabriel faces immediate release. The accelerated departure makes responsibility a practical decision made with incomplete resources. Once outside, the consequences continue through hunger, injury, and care for the baby.

The action is not completed merely by breaking a rule. Setting tests whether his commitment persists when freedom becomes work, supporting an interpretation of maturity as sustained attention to consequences rather than a single dramatic gesture.

A complication worth answering

Look more closely: test the argument

A young person cannot be expected to control an entire social system or guarantee the safety of everyone affected by change. The novel gives Jonas unusual knowledge and an urgent particular obligation, not unlimited competence. An alternative emphasis might question the risks of the plan for people left behind.

That concern is legitimate and makes the Giver’s commitment to support them important. Responsibility requires considering vulnerability on more than one side, even when a decision cannot protect everyone equally.

Model analytical paragraph · paraphrased evidence

The novel presents moral maturity as judging an action by the needs it serves rather than by whether it looks obedient or rebellious. In Chapter 20, Jonas wants the Giver to escape with him, but the older man chooses to remain and help people face returned memories. His decision differs from passive acceptance because its purpose is to support change. Jonas’s departure serves another obligation, which becomes urgent when Gabriel faces release (Ch. 21). The contrast between staying and leaving prevents either movement from becoming a universal symbol of courage. Lowry also extends Jonas’s responsibility beyond the dramatic departure: hunger and injury require continued care in Chapter 22. Acting ethically therefore involves more than announcing disagreement or taking a first risk. It includes accepting burdens afterward and recognizing people whose needs differ from one’s own. Although neither character can guarantee the plan’s outcome, their decisions demonstrate a developing capacity to connect understanding with particular obligations under conditions of uncertainty.

Two misconceptions to correct

Age or suffering automatically makes a person wise.

The novel shows learning through interpretation, relationships, and decisions; experience alone can also overwhelm.

Breaking a rule is enough to demonstrate responsibility.

Analyse the need an action serves and its consequences. Jonas’s ongoing care matters as much as the initial departure.
