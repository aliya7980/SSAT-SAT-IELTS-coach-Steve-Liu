Characters and Relationships

Judge traits through choices, relationships, and the limits of what we can know.

Who matters, and how?

Major characters sustain the central action or development; minor characters can still be essential to a theme. These categories are useful judgements, not official rankings. Jonas is the protagonist. The Giver is his mentor, while Gabriel is central to the action despite having little speech.

Character studies

Jonas · Major · protagonist

Traits and motivation: Careful, observant, increasingly questioning. His desire to understand becomes a willingness to protect Gabriel.

Evidence: Word choice (Ch. 1), selection (Ch. 8), release recording (Ch. 19), escape (Ch. 21).

Narrative role: His development connects knowledge with ethical action, although courage does not remove doubt or danger.

The Giver · Major · mentor and co-agent

Traits and motivation: Knowledgeable, burdened, compassionate; moves from carrying suffering alone toward planning change.

Evidence: Painful training (Chs. 14–15), Rosemary’s history (Ch. 18), decision to remain (Ch. 20).

Narrative role: His mentorship shows that wisdom includes responsibility to people who have not yet learned.

Gabriel · Major in the central action · dependent and catalyst

Traits and motivation: Vulnerable and responsive to care; he receives soothing memories and needs protection.

Evidence: Pale eyes (Ch. 3), shared memory (Ch. 14), threatened release and escape (Ch. 21).

Narrative role: His needs turn Jonas’s objections into urgent personal responsibility; he is not merely an abstract symbol.

Jonas’s father · Important supporting character

Traits and motivation: Attentive and gentle within his assigned role, yet capable of lethal obedience.

Evidence: Extra care for Gabriel (Chs. 1–3), twin recording (Ch. 19).

Narrative role: The contradiction challenges the idea that a pleasant manner guarantees ethical judgement. His full inner understanding is not narrated.

Jonas’s development timeline

  1. Chs. 1–5: accepts routines while noticing puzzles.
  2. Chs. 8–12: receives unusual responsibilities and new perception.
  3. Chs. 13–17: experience creates empathy, frustration, and distance.
  4. Chs. 19–21: knowledge becomes a decision to resist and protect.
  5. Chs. 22–23: acts under uncertainty; responsibility survives the loss of comfort.

Direct and indirect characterization

The Chief Elder directly lists Jonas’s qualities (Ch. 8). His decision to take Gabriel with him indirectly reveals his priorities through action (Ch. 21).

Interpretation: A direct statement can tell readers what a community values; actions let readers judge those values under pressure. The two forms may reinforce or complicate each other.

Discuss: Does the Elder’s account explain everything about the person Jonas becomes?

Write: Compare one named quality with one later action. Explain the relationship instead of simply calling Jonas brave.
