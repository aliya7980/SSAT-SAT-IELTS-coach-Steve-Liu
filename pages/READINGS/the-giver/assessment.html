Assessment and Revision

Read the criteria, examine worked feedback, and choose a revision you can carry out.

A teaching rubric, not an official VSB document

This teacher-created adaptation uses Meaning, Style, Form, and Conventions to assess literary analysis in this unit. It is informed by BC ELA 8 and VSB/Provincial Proficiency Scale guidance. The descriptors below are original classroom adaptations, not official VSB descriptors.

Proficient means meeting Grade 8 expectations. Extending means greater depth, nuance, and purposeful control within the learning—not simply more words, harder vocabulary, or perfect spelling. No percentage conversions are assigned here. MLA requirements are assignment-specific expectations explicitly taught in this unit.

Self-assess with evidence

For each criterion, choose a level and point to a sentence in your draft that supports the choice. Then name one attainable revision. Your judgement is a starting point for feedback, not an automatic grade.

Revision checklist

Revise Meaning and Form before polishing Style and Conventions. Ask a partner to underline the claim, box the evidence, and mark the sentence that most clearly explains the link.

Meaning: the thinking your paragraph develops

Meaning concerns the accuracy, relevance, and depth of the interpretation. A paragraph saying that Jonas is different names a topic but does not explain why the difference matters. A stronger claim argues that Jonas’s search for an accurate word helps reflection while revealing a public language standard inside his private thought.

Accurate evidence and explanation must work together: a long quotation cannot compensate for a claim the passage does not support. Proficient Grade 8 work develops a defensible argument from specific details. Extending work explores a significant complication or alternative without losing focus.

Style: the choices that make the thinking clear

Style is the way a writer uses language for a reader and purpose. In literary analysis, precise verbs such as contrasts, limits, or reveals can explain a relationship more effectively than proves used for every idea. Vocabulary should fit what the passage does; calling every detail symbolism creates confusion rather than sophistication.

Sentence variety can separate an event from its interpretation or emphasize a qualification. Proficient style communicates clearly with appropriate terms. Extending style controls emphasis and complexity purposefully, rather than decorating a weak argument with difficult words.

Form: the arrangement that guides a reader

Form concerns structure and connections. A focused paragraph introduces a claim, provides enough context for evidence, explains how the evidence supports it, and returns to the argument. These are functions rather than a compulsory number of sentences.

An essay also needs an introduction, a logical sequence of developed paragraphs, and a conclusion that reflects the reasoning. Proficient form makes the line of thought easy to follow. Extending form arranges comparisons or counterpoints where they sharpen the argument, instead of adding a final however that the paragraph never explains.

Conventions: the editing that protects meaning

Conventions include sentence boundaries, punctuation, spelling, capitalization, and the documentation expectations explicitly taught for this assignment. For example, a quotation must fit its surrounding sentence, and a citation must identify the passage without a made-up page number. These details matter because readers need to distinguish the student’s wording and ideas from a source.

Proficient work is clear and consistently edited, not necessarily error-free. Extending work handles conventions smoothly and purposefully. The taught MLA expectations belong to this unit’s task; they are not being presented as a universal official VSB rule.
