Vocabulary-Building Strategies

Do not just read a word. Retrieve it, use it, and connect it.

A practical four-stage method for remembering advanced vocabulary and applying it under SSAT and SAT test conditions.

Why vocabulary practice matters on both tests

The SSAT and SAT demand related but different kinds of vocabulary knowledge. Effective preparation must build fast recognition and flexible use.

For the SSAT

The Upper Level SSAT verbal section includes 30 synonym questions and 30 analogy questions in 30 minutes.

  • Students must recognize meanings quickly.
  • They must distinguish close but incorrect choices.
  • They must understand relationships between words and ideas.

For the Digital SAT

SAT Reading and Writing assesses high-utility words and phrases in short passages, especially within Craft and Structure questions.

  • Students must infer meaning from context.
  • They must notice tone, precision, and rhetorical purpose.
  • They must select the word that fits the whole sentence or passage.

The four-exercise memory pathway

Each exercise changes the mental task. That progression prevents the illusion of knowing a word simply because it looks familiar.

1

Word + Definition

Begin by learning the word’s core meaning, part of speech, and one precise distinction. Say the word aloud, cover the definition, and explain it in your own words.

Example: abate (verb) — to become less intense or to reduce.
Why it is efficient: This creates an accurate starting representation. Parts of speech also help students eliminate grammatically impossible SAT choices.
2

Matching

Match words to scrambled definitions without looking back at the study list. Explain why the correct definition fits and why a tempting alternative does not.

Example: abate → “to lessen in strength,” not “to surrender authority.”
Why it is efficient: Matching adds retrieval and discrimination. Those are essential for SSAT synonym questions, where several choices may feel vaguely related.
3

Use the Word in a Sentence

Complete a sentence from a scrambled word bank, then write a second original sentence. The surrounding clues should make the meaning clear.

Example: “The wind began to abate after the storm moved east.”
Why it is efficient: Sentence work connects meaning with grammar, tone, and context—the same information students need for SAT words-in-context questions.
4

Use the Words in a Paragraph

Read a paragraph containing every target word. Then retell its meaning, replace selected words with synonyms, or write a new paragraph using several words together.

Example: A story can connect abate, acrimonious, and acquiesce through one meaningful conflict.
Why it is efficient: Paragraphs create multiple connections among meaning, theme, cause, contrast, and tone. This supports SAT passage comprehension and SSAT analogy reasoning.

A simple spaced-review schedule

Do not finish a list once and abandon it. Short reviews spread across several days make retrieval more durable.

Day 1

Learn

Study word-definition cards. Say each meaning aloud and complete matching once.

Day 2

Retrieve

Redo matching from memory. Review mistakes immediately, then complete sentence gaps.

Day 4

Apply

Complete the scrambled gap exercise again and write original sentences for difficult words.

Day 7

Integrate

Read the all-words paragraph, retell it, and quiz the missed words one final time.

Track difficult wordsSpend more time on misses, not on words already mastered.

Research behind the method

The recommendations above combine official test descriptions with research on retrieval, spacing, and vocabulary in context.
